**VaxForge: an end-to-end reverse vaccinology pipeline that preserves protective antigens under a soft virulence filter and resolves host-specific epitope repertoires**

**Abstract**

Reverse vaccinology tools rank candidate antigens from a genome, but most stop at the ranking step and treat the host as a single reference, which limits both their recall of validated antigens and their utility for veterinary and cross-species targets. We present VaxForge, a reverse vaccinology pipeline that carries a pathogen from candidate discovery through a soft-filter antigen funnel, host-specific class I and class II epitope prediction across five host species, and a characterised multi-epitope construct, and that ends in an in silico immunisation step. We benchmarked VaxForge against two established tools, NERVE 2.0 and Vaxign-ML, under default parameters and identical inputs. Across four bacterial proteomes with fifteen licensed or preclinical protective antigens as ground truth, VaxForge recovered 14 of 15, comparable to Vaxign-ML (15 of 15) and well ahead of NERVE 2.0 (9 of 15), while placing protective antigens at higher ranks (for example iron-regulated surface determinant B at rank 4 of 2,889 proteins). The single antigen VaxForge missed, ActA, also ranked lowest in the independent comparator and is a genuine hard case rather than an artefact. NERVE 2.0 reached higher fold enrichment but only by discarding three of the four licensed meningococcal antigens at every input scale, showing that fold enrichment alone is misleading. On a rabies model and two additional viruses, VaxForge resolved host-specific glycoprotein epitope repertoires that differed substantially between species (pairwise Jaccard overlap below one, and zero in one case), a dimension the comparators cannot address. VaxForge therefore matches the recall of a strong independent predictor while adding host resolution and an end-to-end construct that the field currently lacks.

**Introduction**

Reverse vaccinology began with serogroup B *Neisseria meningitidis*, where candidate antigens were derived directly from the whole-genome sequence rather than from cultured components (Pizza et al., 2000). The approach reshaped bacterial vaccine discovery and produced the antigens behind the licensed four-component meningococcal vaccine, recombinant factor H binding protein, Neisserial adhesin A and Neisserial Heparin Binding Antigen together with a PorA-bearing outer-membrane vesicle (Giuliani et al., 2006). Two decades later the paradigm is served by mature software. NERVE 2.0 provides a current, locally installable reverse vaccinology environment (Conte et al., 2024), and Vaxign-ML applies supervised machine learning to predict bacterial protective antigens (Ong et al., 2020).

These tools share three limitations that matter in practice. First, a hard virulence or localisation filter can raise the apparent enrichment of a candidate list while silently discarding true antigens; the Neisserial Heparin Binding Antigen, for instance, is absent from curated virulence databases yet is a licensed vaccine component, so any pipeline that requires a virulence-database hit will lose it. Second, epitope-level output is usually computed against a single human reference, which leaves the many veterinary and zoonotic targets, where cattle, pigs or mice are the relevant hosts, without an honest presentation model. Third, most tools deliver a ranked protein list and stop there, so the downstream steps that turn an antigen into an immunogen, epitope selection, construct assembly and a first immunological check, remain manual.

VaxForge was built to close these three gaps within one deterministic pipeline. It replaces the hard virulence filter with a soft, weighted one, so that a genuine surface antigen with no virulence-database annotation can still pass. It predicts class I and class II binding for a user-selected host drawn from human, mouse, bovine, porcine and avian allele panels, so that the epitope repertoire reflects the intended species. And it continues past ranking into a multi-epitope construct and an in silico immunisation readout, giving an end-to-end path from proteome to candidate immunogen. Here we quantify the first two contributions against NERVE 2.0 and Vaxign-ML on four bacteria and three viruses, and we report a pipeline validation that surfaced and fixed two scale-dependent defects during the work.

**Results**

**The VaxForge pipeline.** VaxForge processes a proteome, coding sequences or a raw genome through automatic input detection, then discovery, an antigen funnel, host-aware epitope prediction, construct assembly and an immunisation check. Discovery aligns proteins against curated virulence references with DIAMOND (Buchfink et al., 2021) and the VFDB (Liu et al., 2022). The funnel combines subcellular localisation from PSORTb (Yu et al., 2010) and DeepLoc (Thumuluri et al., 2022), transmembrane topology from TMHMM (Krogh et al., 2001), signal peptides from SignalP (Almagro Armenteros et al., 2019), antigenicity from IApred (Miles et al., 2025) and a mandatory human-homology exclusion for autoimmune safety, applying a small number of hard cutoffs and ranking the remainder by a weighted candidacy score. Surviving proteins pass to epitope prediction, where NetMHCpan and NetMHCIIpan (Reynisson et al., 2020) score class I and class II binding for the chosen host, complemented by class I immunogenicity (Calis et al., 2013), antigen processing (Larsen et al., 2005) and B-cell epitope prediction with BepiPred-3.0 (Clifford et al., 2022). Selected epitopes are assembled into a characterised multi-epitope construct and passed to the in silico immunisation module. Every method is reported with its citation so that the provenance of each score is explicit.

**VaxForge recovers all four licensed meningococcal antigens where NERVE 2.0 recovers one.** We first benchmarked on serogroup B *N. meningitidis* (strain MC58, proteome UP000000425), with the four 4CMenB antigens as ground truth. On an expanded 204-protein set (four protective plus 200 background, fixed seed), VaxForge recovered all four antigens (recall 4 of 4; 68 candidates; fold enrichment 3.12; Fisher exact p = 0.012), ranking PorA first, factor H binding protein third, the Heparin Binding Antigen sixth and adhesin A eighth. Vaxign-ML also recovered all four (59 candidates), whereas NERVE 2.0 recovered only PorA (recall 1 of 4). The same pattern held on a compact 64-protein set and on the full 2,003-protein proteome for the two faster tools, where Vaxign-ML again recovered all four and NERVE 2.0 recovered one (Figures 1 and 2). The decisive case is the Heparin Binding Antigen, which is virulence-database negative: VaxForge retained it through the soft funnel, while NERVE 2.0 removed it under its hard selection step. That an independent machine-learning tool, Vaxign-ML, reached the same 4 of 4 recall indicates the behaviour is a property of soft filtering rather than a single-tool artefact.

**High fold enrichment does not imply high recall.** NERVE 2.0 produced short candidate lists with high fold enrichment (25 on the 204 set, 62.5 on the full proteome), consistent with its published value near 16.6, yet its Fisher exact p was not significant because the candidate count was very small, and it missed three of the four licensed antigens at every scale (Figure 3). The three losses, factor H binding protein, adhesin A and the Heparin Binding Antigen, were not caused by low continuous scores from NERVE 2.0 itself, which rated them highly, but by the hard selection step applied after scoring (periplasmic localisation, adhesin probability below the 0.5 cutoff, and an unknown-localisation call with one predicted transmembrane helix). Fold enrichment therefore rewards selectivity and can rise precisely as recall falls, so it is misleading when read on its own. The metric that reflects what a tool actually delivers is recall over known antigens together with the ranks of those antigens.

**The pattern generalises across genome scale, and a validation step made the results trustworthy.** To move beyond a single exemplar we ran three additional zoonotic bacteria at full proteome scale, chosen for increasing genome size and Gram balance: *Staphylococcus aureus* NCTC 8325 (2.8 Mb, 2,889 proteins, Gram positive), *Listeria monocytogenes* EGD-e (2.9 Mb, 2,844 proteins, Gram positive) and *Salmonella enterica* serovar Typhimurium LT2 (4.9 Mb, 4,533 proteins, Gram negative), each with five literature-supported protective or candidate antigens (`PROTECTIVE_ANTIGENS.md`). VaxForge recovered 5 of 5 for *S. aureus* (729 candidates; fold 3.98; AUC 1.00; iron-regulated surface determinant B at rank 4), 4 of 5 for *L. monocytogenes* (478 candidates; fold 4.79) and 5 of 5 for *S.* Typhimurium (1,368 candidates; AUC 0.99), for a total of 14 of 15 (Figure 5). Vaxign-ML recovered 15 of 15 and NERVE 2.0 recovered 9 of 15, missing the same kinds of surface antigens it lost on meningococcus. VaxForge placed protective antigens at higher ranks than Vaxign-ML on the two species where both reached full recall. The single VaxForge miss, ActA of *L. monocytogenes*, is a genuine hard antigen: it also ranked at the bottom of the Vaxign-ML list, so it reflects the difficulty of the target rather than a pipeline fault. During these runs we identified and corrected two scale-dependent engineering defects that had artefactually lowered VaxForge recall in first-pass runs (initial values of 3 of 5, 3 of 5 and 0 of 5); the localisation and MHC steps were each collapsing under a single oversized batch at full-proteome scale, and both were repartitioned into bounded, parallel sub-batches (`MANUSCRIPT_METHODS.md`, section B). Control experiments confirmed that the toxicity and allergenicity filters were not responsible.

**Host selection changes the predicted epitope repertoire, a dimension the comparators cannot address.** The viral arm was built to exercise host-specific presentation. Using rabies virus (RefSeq NC_001542), whose glycoprotein G is the only structural protein that elicits neutralising, protective antibodies (Cox et al., 1977), VaxForge ranked G second of five proteins and, more importantly, produced different bound-epitope sets for each of four natural mammalian hosts (glycoprotein G: 8 epitopes in mouse, 7 in human, 7 in bovine, 5 in pig). Pairwise host overlap of the glycoprotein G epitopes was well below unity (Jaccard 0.75 for human and bovine, 0.67 for human and mouse, 0.50 for human and pig), so the choice of host materially changed the predicted repertoire. Two further four-host viruses reproduced the effect: vesicular stomatitis virus, a rhabdovirus relative of rabies, again placed glycoprotein G at rank 2 of 5 with human and pig overlap as low as 0.33, and encephalomyocarditis virus placed capsid VP1 at rank 3 of 11 with no shared epitopes at all between bovine and pig (Jaccard 0.00). NERVE 2.0 is bacterial only and Vaxign-ML has no host dimension (it ranked rabies glycoprotein G fourth of five with no host resolution), so this capability is unique to VaxForge among the three. Because rabies presents a single protective antigen, the rank statistic here is descriptive rather than inferential (permutation p = 0.40, n = 1), and population-coverage data were reported as unavailable for non-human hosts rather than imputed.

---

**Discussion**

The central result is that VaxForge matches a strong independent predictor on the metric that matters for a reverse vaccinology tool, recovery of known protective antigens, while adding two things that tool does not provide. Across four bacteria VaxForge recovered 14 of 15 validated antigens against 15 of 15 for Vaxign-ML and 9 of 15 for NERVE 2.0, and it placed those antigens at competitive or higher ranks. The consistent shortfall of NERVE 2.0 is instructive: its hard selection step removed genuine surface antigens that its own continuous score had rated highly, which inflated fold enrichment while suppressing recall. A pipeline that reports fold enrichment without recall can therefore look sharper than it is, and we would encourage readers of reverse vaccinology benchmarks to weight recall over known antigens accordingly.

Two features distinguish VaxForge beyond recall. The soft virulence filter recovered the Neisserial Heparin Binding Antigen, a licensed component with no virulence-database annotation, and the host-aware epitope layer produced species-specific repertoires for rabies, vesicular stomatitis and encephalomyocarditis viruses. The latter is relevant to the large veterinary and zoonotic space where the intended host is not human, and where a single human reference would misstate which peptides are presented. VaxForge also continues past ranking into construct assembly and an in silico immunisation readout, so the output is a candidate immunogen rather than a list.

Several limitations bound these claims and are stated plainly. ActA was missed by VaxForge, and although it is a genuine hard antigen that the independent tool also ranked last, its recovery would require either conformational or context-specific signals that a linear-epitope pipeline does not model. The default toxicity filter can remove peptides derived from toxin antigens, which lowered peptide-level recall for the pore-forming listeriolysin O before the scale fixes and remains a conservative choice we did not tune for the benchmark. The area-under-curve figures are reported as secondary with an explicit small-sample caveat rather than as a headline, because with only four to five positives per pathogen the differences are not statistically resolvable, and we make no claim of superior discrimination on that basis. The bacterial comparison also carries a scale asymmetry: VaxForge full-proteome meningococcal runs are impractical on the current CPU-only hardware, so the flagship meningococcal comparison used matched subsampled sets that are fair across tools, while whole-proteome runs were available only for the faster comparators. Finally, roughly nine tenths of B-cell epitopes are discontinuous, and the linear construct produced here does not represent conformational epitopes, a scope limit we note rather than resolve.

Two directions follow directly. A GPU backend would allow whole-proteome VaxForge runs and complete the head-to-head at full scale for every pathogen, and the host-specific epitope predictions, particularly the cross-species divergence seen for the viral glycoproteins, are concrete hypotheses that binding and immunisation experiments could test. The pipeline and its benchmark are structured so that both extensions slot into the existing framework without redesign.
